SLS technology: how it works, steps, applications, advantages and limits

A part comes out of an SLS 3D printer as if from a block of powder. Nothing holds it up, no visible scaffolding, just a solid shape hidden in a bed of fine material. That is one of the great strengths of this technology: it can produce complex objects without support structures.

SLS technology, for Selective Laser Sintering, is one of the most widely used 3D printing methods for producing strong prototypes, small series and some end-use parts. It is based on a principle that is simple to understand but very precise to master: a laser heats a powder until its particles bond together, layer by layer.

The part gradually emerges from the powder after manufacturing.

SLS is based on a powder and a laser

SLS belongs to the family of powder bed fusion processes. Unlike a filament 3D printer, which deposits molten material through a nozzle, an SLS machine works with a tank full of powder. This powder becomes the raw material of the part.

The general operation follows a logic repeated hundreds or thousands of times:

  • A thin layer of powder is spread over a platform.
  • A laser traces the shape of the part on this layer.
  • The heated particles bond together.
  • The platform lowers slightly.
  • A new layer of powder is deposited.
  • The cycle repeats until the complete object is formed.

The term "sintering" refers to bonding particles with heat, without always fully liquefying the material as in conventional casting. With the polymers used in SLS, the material can reach a state very close to local melting. The grains soften, fuse together and create a solid zone.

The powder not reached by the laser stays in place. It surrounds the part during printing and acts as a natural support. This is what makes internal shapes, channels, integrated hinges or lattice structures possible, which would be much harder to produce with other methods.

The most common materials are polymer powders, in particular polyamide, often called nylon. PA12 is very widespread, as it offers a good balance of strength, precision and stability. There are also PA11, powders filled with fibres or glass beads, and flexible materials such as some TPUs. For metals, similar processes such as laser powder bed fusion are often mentioned, but SLS is mainly associated with engineering plastics.

The steps of the SLS process go well beyond printing

The success of an SLS part does not depend on the laser alone. It relies on a whole chain of preparation, settings, cooling and finishing. Each step affects the final quality.

Preparing the digital model

It all starts with a 3D file. The part is designed in CAD software, then exported in a format the machine can read. The file is then prepared by slicing software.

This software cuts the model into very thin layers. It also calculates the path the laser will follow. In an SLS machine, the operator can place several parts in the same build volume. This is often called nesting or filling the build. Because the powder supports the objects, parts can be stacked in the available space, as long as the necessary distances between them are kept.

This step has a direct effect on cost and lead time. A well-filled build makes better use of the machine and reduces wasted space.

Preparing the powder

The powder must be clean, dry and uniform. If it contains too much moisture or clumped grains, quality can drop. Parts may then show surface defects, less consistent strength or poorly fused areas.

Before printing, the powder may be sieved. In many workshops, part of the unsintered powder is recovered after manufacturing and mixed with fresh powder. This mix must remain controlled, as powder that has aged too much can behave differently under heat.

Particle size also matters. Fine grains favour detail but must remain easy to spread. A powder that is too irregular gives less uniform layers.

The consistency of the powder affects the precision of the layers.

Preheating the chamber

The machine heats the entire build volume to a temperature close to the point where the powder starts to bond. This preheating is essential. It reduces the energy the laser has to supply and limits thermal stress.

If the temperature difference between the areas processed by the laser and the rest of the powder is too great, the parts can warp. Thermal control is therefore one of the most sensitive aspects of SLS.

The laser pass and the fusion of the particles

Once the powder layer has been spread, the laser starts moving. It does not sweep the whole surface at random. It follows precisely the contours and solid areas defined in the digital file.

In polymer machines, the laser is often a CO₂ laser. It delivers concentrated energy to the powder. The particles absorb this energy, heat up quickly and fuse with their neighbours. The machine adjusts several parameters:

  • Laser power
  • Scanning speed
  • The spacing between two passes
  • Layer thickness
  • Powder bed temperature

These settings must match the material. Too little energy causes poor sintering: the part becomes fragile or porous. Too much energy can burn the material, round off details or cause warping.

Cooling and extracting the parts

When printing is finished, the parts do not come out straight away. The powder block must cool slowly. This phase may seem passive, but it protects the geometry of the parts. Cooling too quickly increases the risk of warping.

After cooling, the operator removes the "cake", the volume of powder containing the parts. The objects are freed by hand or with suitable equipment. The loose powder is recovered, then the parts go through depowdering.

Post-manufacturing finishes

A raw SLS part often has a slightly grainy surface. Depending on its use, it can be used as is or given a finish:

  • Bead blasting to improve the surface appearance
  • Dyeing to obtain a uniform colour
  • Polishing or chemical smoothing depending on the material
  • Local machining for precise functional surfaces
  • Inserting threaded elements or metal pins

These finishes turn a raw technical part into a component ready for assembly.

SLS applications span many sectors

SLS has established itself in professional 3D printing because it produces strong, functional parts without supports. It isn't just for models. It is used in design, testing, production and maintenance.

Functional prototyping

Design offices use SLS to test shapes, assemblies and mechanisms. An SLS nylon part can be screwed, clipped, handled and fitted into a prototype. It stands up to testing better than a purely visual model.

For example, a manufacturer can print a housing, a handle or a hinge to check ergonomics before launching an injection mould. This reduces back-and-forth and helps detect problems earlier.

Small-series manufacturing

SLS is well suited to limited runs. When a few dozen or a few hundred parts are needed, creating a mould can cost too much or take too long. Additive manufacturing can then produce the parts directly.

This approach is used for customised components, technical accessories, spare parts or niche products. It also helps keep production flexible, without storing large volumes.

Tooling and production aids

In industry, SLS also produces jigs, inspection fixtures, drilling guides and guards. These objects are not always visible in the final product, but they make work in the workshop easier.

A light, strong SLS part can replace a heavier machined element. It can also incorporate shapes tailored to a specific workstation.

Tooling printed in SLS can be light, strong and tailored to a specific use.

Medical, transport and consumer goods

In the medical field, SLS can be used to produce orthoses, anatomical models or components tailored to an individual. These uses require specific materials and validations, but the ability to customise remains a major asset.

In transport, the technology is used to create prototypes of ducts, brackets, housings or lightweight parts. In consumer goods, it is used to test glasses, accessories, cases or parts with shapes that are hard to mould.

SLS is also useful for internal geometries. Networks of channels, lattices or lightweight structures can reduce mass without removing all the strength.

The advantages of SLS technology are tangible

SLS technology is attractive because it combines freedom of form with mechanical performance. Its benefits are not theoretical. They show in the way parts are designed and produced.

Great geometric freedom

The powder supports the part, which avoids most supports and allows complex shapes.

Batch production

Several parts can be placed in the same volume, which makes small-series production efficient.

Good repeatability

With well-controlled parameters, the technology gives consistent results from one batch to the next.

Functional parts

Materials such as nylon produce strong components, flexible in some cases and suitable for testing.

Fewer assemblies

A part can incorporate clips, hinges, housings, channels or internal structures from the design stage.

No supports to remove

Post-processing focuses mainly on depowdering and finishing.

Design freedom is probably the best-known advantage. It lets you design a part for its function rather than for the constraints of a mould or a cutting tool. That doesn't mean anything goes without rules. Minimum thicknesses, assembly clearances and powder removal must be planned for.

SLS also helps eliminate certain steps. A part that would have required several assembled components can sometimes be printed in one go. This can simplify maintenance and reduce weak points.

The limits of SLS must be taken seriously

SLS isn't the ideal answer for every project. It offers a lot of possibilities, but it also imposes technical, economic and practical constraints.

The first limit concerns surface appearance. A raw SLS part doesn't have the smooth finish of a part injected in a polished mould. Its texture is fine but grainy. For parts that are visible or handled by customers, an additional finish may be needed.

Precision is good, but it depends on geometry, material and cooling. Large flat parts can warp. Thin areas can be fragile. Very tight tolerances sometimes require machining after printing.

Cycle time must also be considered. Printing can be long, and cooling adds further time. For a one-off part, this is usually acceptable. For very large volumes, plastic injection generally remains more suitable once the mould has paid for itself.

Powder handling requires rigour. It must be stored, sieved and mixed carefully, and operators must be protected from inhaling dust. Professional machines use systems designed for this, but the working environment must remain controlled.

Finally, the choice of materials remains more limited than with other manufacturing processes. Nylon covers many needs, but it doesn't replace all engineering plastics or all metals.

Surface texture is one of the points to assess before choosing SLS.

When to choose SLS for a manufacturing project

SLS is a good choice when the part must be functional, complex and produced in low or medium quantities. It is particularly relevant if supports would be a problem with another 3D printing technology.

It suits projects that require:

  • Complex or internal geometries
  • Strong parts in engineering polymer
  • Prototypes close to end use
  • Small series without a mould
  • Customised components
  • Lightweight workshop tooling

It is less suitable if the main requirement is a very smooth surface straight off the machine, a very low cost in very large series, or a specific material not available as SLS powder.

The right reflex is to start from the end use. Must the part withstand impacts, bending, heat, humidity or repeated friction? Must it be attractive, precise, light, watertight, flexible? The answers guide the choice of material, technology and finishing.

SLS shows its full value when it is built in from the design stage. A part simply copied from a drawing intended for machining doesn't always benefit from the process. By rethinking shapes, recesses, assemblies and integrated functions, it becomes possible to create a lighter, simpler and sometimes more effective part.

SLS technology is therefore neither a laboratory curiosity nor a magic solution. It is a powerful industrial tool based on a clear idea: turning powder into a solid object with a precise laser. Used well, it opens up very useful options for prototyping, manufacturing and customising parts. The best result comes from a balance between design, material, machine settings and finishing.
